LIT-SiGamal

Long Isogeny with Torsion based

Published

July 12, 2026

Modified

July 12, 2026

1 Overview

LIT-SiGamal is a public key encryption (PKE) scheme that constructs an ElGamal-like mechanism over supersingular elliptic curve isogenies by combining the structural approach of SiGamal with the Long Isogeny with Torsion (LIT) diagram framework [MoSt25]. The original SiGamal scheme relied on a commutative CSIDH-based diagram to enable the sender and receiver to compute parallel isogenies without revealing their secret paths. However, CSIDH-based constructions require computing group actions over extremely large primes (often exceeding 3,000 bits for baseline security), rendering them highly inefficient. LIT-SiGamal bypasses this bottleneck by replacing the CSIDH diagram with a LIT diagram, a commutative diagram parameterized to consist of large-degree horizontal isogenies and relatively low-degree vertical isogenies. To make the bottom isogeny computable without knowledge of the vertical secret isogenies, LIT-SiGamal incorporates auxiliary torsion points masked by matrices, a technique adapted from the FESTA scheme. This design allows for significantly smaller primes and faster execution times while maintaining robust post-quantum security. Moreover, the constructions of POKÉ and LIT-SiGamal are nearly identical, with the key distinction being the degree of the isogeny used in the public key: POKÉ employs an isogeny of degree approximately \(2^{2\lambda}\), whereas LIT-SiGamal uses one of degree approximately \(2^{6\lambda}\).

2 Scheme Design

The LIT-SiGamal protocol operates over a carefully structured prime field and relies on the exchange of evaluated torsion bases to enable a shared commutative diagram.

2.1 Public Parameters

  • Prime Characteristic: The scheme operates over \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\) where the prime \(p\) is of the form \(p = \ell_A^a \ell_B^b \ell_C^c f - 1\). Here, \(\ell_A\), \(\ell_B\), and \(\ell_C\) are pairwise coprime small integers, and \(f\) is a small positive cofactor.
  • Matrix Group (\(\mathcal{M}_a\)): The subgroup of diagonal matrices within \(GL_2(\mathbb{Z}/\ell_A^a\mathbb{Z})\).
  • Plaintext Space (\(\mathcal{P}\)): The unit group of \(\mathbb{Z}/\ell_C^c\mathbb{Z}\), meaning \(\mathcal{P} = (\mathbb{Z}/\ell_C^c\mathbb{Z})^\times\).
  • Security Parameter (\(\lambda\)): Determines the exact exponents \(a, b, c\) such that \(\ell_B^b \approx 2^{2\lambda}\), \(\ell_C^c \approx 2^\lambda\), and \(\ell_A^{2a} \approx \ell_B^{2b} \cdot 2^{2\lambda}\).

2.2 Key Generation (Receiver)

Receiver’s goal is to establish the top horizontal isogeny of the LIT diagram and publicize its action on specific torsion subgroups.

  1. Curve and Isogeny Generation: Generates a pair of supersingular elliptic curves \((E_0, E_1)\) over \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\) along with a cyclic isogeny \(\phi_1: E_0 \rightarrow E_1\) of large degree \(\ell_A^{2a} - n^2\). The integer \(n\) is chosen such that \(\ell_A^{2a} - n^2 > \ell_B^{2b} \cdot 2^{2\lambda}\).
  2. Torsion Bases: Defines \(\mathcal{B}_A=(P_A,Q_A)\) as an ordered basis of \(E_0[\ell_A^a]\), and \(\mathcal{B}_B = (P_B, Q_B)\) as an ordered basis of \(E_0[\ell_B^b]\).
  3. Auxiliary Point: Selects a random point \(R \in E_0\) of order \(\ell_C^c\).
  4. Masking: Samples a random masking matrix \(\mathbf{A} \in \mathcal{M}_a\) and a random scalar \(\alpha \in (\mathbb{Z}/\ell_C^c\mathbb{Z})^\times\).
  5. Evaluation: Computes the masked image of the torsion basis as \({}^t\mathcal{B}_1 := \mathbf{A} \cdot {}^t\phi_1(\mathcal{B}_A)\) and the scaled image of the auxiliary point as \(R_1 := \alpha\phi_1(R)\).
  6. Keys:
    • Public Key: \((E_0, \mathcal{B}_A, \mathcal{B}_B, R), (E_1, \mathcal{B}_1, \phi_1(\mathcal{B}_B), R_1)\).
    • Secret Key: The masking matrix and scalar \((\mathbf{A}, \alpha)\).

2.3 Encryption (Sender)

Sender embeds his plaintext \(\mu \in (\mathbb{Z}/\ell_C^c\mathbb{Z})^\times\) by pushing the public parameters through random vertical \(\ell_B^b\)-isogenies.

  1. Secret Isogeny Kernels: Sender samples \(s \in (\mathbb{Z}/\ell_B^b\mathbb{Z})^\times\), which defines the kernels for his vertical isogenies. Computes \(\phi_2: E_0 \rightarrow E_0' := E_0/\langle P_B + sQ_B \rangle\) and \(\phi_2': E_1 \rightarrow E_1' := E_1/\langle \phi_1(P_B) + s\phi_1(Q_B) \rangle\).
  2. Masking: Samples his own random masking matrix \(\mathbf{B} \in \mathcal{M}_a\) and scalar \(\beta \in (\mathbb{Z}/\ell_C^c\mathbb{Z})^\times\).
  3. Evaluation: Pushes receiver’s bases through his vertical isogenies and masks them:
    • \({}^t\mathcal{B}_2 := \mathbf{B} \cdot {}^t\phi_2(\mathcal{B}_A)\).
    • \({}^t\mathcal{B}_3 := \mathbf{B} \cdot {}^t\phi_2'(\mathcal{B}_1)\).
  4. Message Embedding: Scales the torsion points: \(R' := \beta\phi_2(R)\) and embeds the plaintext into the target point: \(R_1' := \mu\beta\phi_2'(R_1)\).
  5. Ciphertext: Sender outputs \((E_0', \mathcal{B}_2, R'), (E_1', \mathcal{B}_3, R_1')\).

2.4 Decryption (Receiver)

Receiver must recover the bottom horizontal isogeny \(\phi_1': E_0' \rightarrow E_1'\) to unmask the plaintext, without knowing sender’s vertical isogenies.

  1. Unmasking: Uses secret matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) to compute \({}^t(P_2', Q_2') := \mathbf{A} \cdot {}^t\mathcal{B}_2\), where \(\mathcal{B}_2 = (P_2,Q_2)\). Also scales the intermediate point \(R'' := \alpha R'\).
  2. Higher-Dimensional Recovery: Using techniques originally developed for SIDH attacks, constructs two 2-dimensional isogenies from \(E_0' \times E_1'\) to an abelian surface \(V\).
    • \(\Psi_0: E_0' \times E_1' \rightarrow V\) with kernel \(\langle (nP_2', P_3), (nQ_2', Q_3) \rangle\)
    • \(\Psi_1: E_0' \times E_1' \rightarrow V\) with kernel \(\langle (nP_2', -P_3), (nQ_2', -Q_3) \rangle\)
    where \((P_3, Q_3) := \mathcal{B}_3\).
  3. Evaluating the Hidden Isogeny: The map \(\phi_1'\) is implicitly captured within these dimension-2 isogenies via Kani’s theorem. Recovers the un-blinded target point \(R_1'' := \pi_2 \circ \hat{\Psi}_1 \circ \Psi_0((-R'', 0))\), where \(\pi_2\) is the projection \(E_0'\times E_1'\to E_1'\).
  4. Message Extraction: Finally, using Pohlig-Hellman, solves the discrete logarithm problem in the smooth subgroup of order \(\ell_C^c\) to find \(\mu'\) such that \(R_1' = \mu' R_1''\), outputting \(\mu'\) as the plaintext.

3 Security Assumptions

The security of LIT-SiGamal relies on variants of the isogeny problem structured around torsion point revelations.

  • LIT-DDH Assumption: The primary assumption under which LIT-SiGamal is proven IND-CPA secure is the LIT-DDH assumption. This is a decisional assumption analogous to the standard Decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH) assumption, but formulated specifically for the tuples generated within a LIT diagram. It asserts that an adversary cannot computationally distinguish between a valid completed commutative isogeny square (with associated evaluated torsion points) and a randomized tuple.
  • CIST Problem: The vertical isogenies executed by the encryptor are protected by the Hardness of the CIST problem. The CIST problem assumes that extracting the secret isogeny is hard when the images of the torsion points are masked by an unknown matrix from a structured subgroup. Because sender masks their torsion evaluations with the matrix \(\mathbf{B} \in \mathcal{M}_a\), an attacker is prevented from performing direct torsion-based attacks to reveal \(\phi_2\) and \(\phi_2'\).
  • LIT Problem: The top horizontal isogeny \(\phi_1\) is protected by the LIT (Long Isogeny with Torsion) problem. In the LIT problem setting, torsion points are explicitly revealed (unmasked), but the degree of the isogeny \(d_1\) is strictly required to be significantly larger than the order of the revealed torsion subgroup (\(d_1 > d_2^2\)). By choosing the horizontal isogeny degree \(\ell_A^{2a} - n^2 \gg \ell_B^{2b} \cdot 2^{2\lambda}\), LIT-SiGamal ensures that the revealed \(\phi_1(\mathcal{B}_B)\) does not leak enough information to mount an attack against \(\phi_1\).

While LIT-SiGamal achieves IND-CPA security under these assumptions, it is not inherently IND-CCA secure due to malleability in the ciphertext components (similar to the original SiGamal). To achieve IND-CCA security for practical KEM use, the scheme must be combined with standard transformations such as the Fujisaki-Okamoto transform.
