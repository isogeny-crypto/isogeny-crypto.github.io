SIDH

Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman

Published

June 12, 2026

Modified

July 11, 2026

1 Overview

It was a NIST PQC competition candidate but is now broken by a polynomial time classical algorithm.

The Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman (SIDH) scheme is a public-key exchange scheme designed to be secure against quantum computer attacks. Diffie-Hellman-type schemes inherently require commutativity to establish a shared secret. SIDH overcomes the noncommutativity of the supersingular endomorphism ring by having parties transmit the images of specific torsion bases under their secret isogenies as auxiliary input [JaFe11].

2 Scheme Design

The scheme dictates that Alice and Bob conduct random walks on two distinct isogeny graphs (of degrees \(\ell_A\) and \(\ell_B\) respectively) and use auxiliary points to arrive at an isomorphic destination curve.

2.1 Public Parameters Setup

  • The scheme is defined over a finite field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\) where the prime \(p\) is of the form \(p = \ell_A^{e_A} \ell_B^{e_B} \cdot f \pm 1\). Here, \(\ell_A\) and \(\ell_B\) are small primes (typically 2 and 3), and \(f\) is a small cofactor.
  • A base supersingular curve \(E_0\) defined over \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\) is fixed as a public parameter.
  • Two sets of bases are published: \(\{P_A, Q_A\}\), which generates the torsion group \(E_0[\ell_A^{e_A}]\), and \(\{P_B, Q_B\}\), which generates \(E_0[\ell_B^{e_B}]\).

2.2 Alice’s Key Generation

  • Alice chooses two random elements \(m_A, n_A \in \mathbb{Z}/\ell_A^{e_A}\mathbb{Z}\), ensuring they are not both divisible by \(\ell_A\).
  • She computes a secret isogeny \(\phi_A : E_0 \rightarrow E_A\) characterized by the kernel \(K_A := \langle [m_A]P_A + [n_A]Q_A \rangle\).
  • To allow Bob to complete the exchange, she evaluates her secret isogeny on Bob’s basis points, yielding \(\phi_A(P_B)\) and \(\phi_A(Q_B)\).
  • Alice’s public key sent to Bob is the tuple \((E_A, \phi_A(P_B), \phi_A(Q_B))\).

2.3 Bob’s Key Generation

  • Bob correspondingly selects random elements \(m_B, n_B \in \mathbb{Z}/\ell_B^{e_B}\mathbb{Z}\).
  • He computes his secret isogeny \(\phi_B : E_0 \rightarrow E_B\) defined by the kernel \(K_B := \langle [m_B]P_B + [n_B]Q_B \rangle\).
  • Bob evaluates his isogeny on Alice’s basis points to produce \(\phi_B(P_A)\) and \(\phi_B(Q_A)\).
  • Bob’s public key sent to Alice is the tuple \((E_B, \phi_B(P_A), \phi_B(Q_A))\).

2.4 Shared Key Derivation

  • Upon receiving Bob’s public key, Alice calculates a new isogeny \(\phi'_A : E_B \rightarrow E_{AB}\) utilizing the evaluated points Bob sent. Her new kernel is \(\langle [m_A]\phi_B(P_A) + [n_A]\phi_B(Q_A) \rangle\).
  • Concurrently, Bob computes a new isogeny \(\phi'_B : E_A \rightarrow E_{BA}\) utilizing Alice’s evaluated points, with the kernel \(\langle [m_B]\phi_A(P_B) + [n_B]\phi_A(Q_B) \rangle\).
  • The curves \(E_{AB}\) and \(E_{BA}\) are isomorphic, allowing both parties to establish a common shared key by computing the \(j\)-invariant \(j(E_{AB})\).

3 Security Assumptions

  • Computational Supersingular Isogeny (CSSI) problem: A unique characteristic of SIDH is the publication of the evaluated torsion basis points (the auxiliary input required for commutativity). The authors state that providing these outputs does not appear to make the underlying problem of finding the secret isogenies any easier.

  • Supersingular Decision Diffie-Hellman (SSDDH) problem: The SSDDH problem tests whether an adversary can successfully distinguish the valid shared secret curve \(E_{AB}\) (derived from the actual secret kernels) from a completely random curve \(E_C\), when given both parties’ public curves and evaluated auxiliary points.

  • Resistance to Quantum Group Actions: Because the scheme is not fundamentally based on an abelian group action, it is completely immune to the subexponential quantum attacks that rendered ordinary isogeny networks insecure.
