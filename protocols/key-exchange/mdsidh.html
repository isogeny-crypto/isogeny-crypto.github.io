MD-SIDH

Masked-degree SIDH

Published

July 8, 2026

Modified

July 8, 2026

1 Overview

The Masked-Degree Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman (MD-SIDH) protocol is a countermeasure designed to secure the SIDH key exchange against devastating torsion point attacks. In the original SIDH protocol, the fixed degrees of the secret isogenies, combined with exact torsion point images, allow attackers to embed the secret isogeny into a higher-dimensional isogeny problem and break the scheme. MD-SIDH mitigates this by masking the degree of the secret isogeny [FoMoPe23]. Specifically, the degrees of the secret isogenies are no longer fixed public parameters, but are instead chosen as uniformly random divisors of public smooth integers. To prevent the efficient recovery of this secret degree using Weil pairing computations and discrete logarithms, the protocol requires masking the images of the torsion points by scaling them with a random invertible integer. This ensures the true degree remains hidden, preserving the security of the protocol while facilitating a successful key exchange.

2 Protocol Design

The MD-SIDH protocol relies on algebraic structures similar SIDH, but introduces variable secret degrees and masking scalars to obscure the exact torsion point mappings and isogeny degrees.

2.1 Public Parameters

The global parameters dictate the structure of the underlying finite fields and curves. Let \(\lambda\) be the target security parameter and let \(t = t(\lambda) \in \mathbb{N}\) be an integer dependent on \(\lambda\).

  • Isogeny Degree Bounds (\(A\) and \(B\)): Let \(A = l_1^{a_1}\cdots l_t^{a_t}\) and \(B = q_1^{b_1}\cdots q_t^{b_t}\) be two smooth, coprime integers.
  • Characteristic (\(p\)): Let \(p = ABf - 1\) be a prime, where \(f\) is a small cofactor. The parameters are chosen such that \(A \approx B\).
  • Starting Curve (\(E_0\)): Let \(E_0\) be a supersingular elliptic curve defined over \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\).
  • Torsion Bases: The bases for the \(A\)-torsion and \(B\)-torsion subgroups are fixed publicly as \(E_0[A] = \langle P_A, Q_A \rangle\) and \(E_0[B] = \langle P_B, Q_B \rangle\).

The complete public parameters are \((E_0, p, A, B, P_A, Q_A, P_B, Q_B)\).

2.2 Key Generation and Exchange

During this phase, Alice and Bob generate their secret isogenies using random divisors of the bounds, mask the resulting torsion points with random invertible scalars, and transmit them.

Alice’s Operations:

  1. Secret Key: Alice samples a uniformly random divisor \(A'\) of \(A\) and a random point \(R_A \in E_0[A']\). Her secret key is \(sk_A = R_A\).
  2. Masking Scalar: Alice samples a uniformly random integer \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}/B\mathbb{Z}^\times\). The integer \(\alpha\) can be deleted immediately after the public key is generated.
  3. Isogeny Computation: She computes the isogeny \(\phi_A: E_0 \rightarrow E_A := E_0/\langle R_A \rangle\).
  4. Public Key: Alice masks her output torsion points by computing \([\alpha]\phi_A(P_B)\) and \([\alpha]\phi_A(Q_B)\). She publishes her public key as \(pk_A = (E_A, [\alpha]\phi_A(P_B), [\alpha]\phi_A(Q_B))\).

Bob’s Operations:

  1. Secret Key: Bob samples a uniformly random divisor \(B'\) of \(B\) and a random point \(R_B \in E_0[B']\). His secret key is \(sk_B = R_B\).
  2. Masking Scalar: Bob samples a uniformly random integer \(\beta \in \mathbb{Z}/A\mathbb{Z}^\times\). The integer \(\beta\) can be deleted immediately after use.
  3. Isogeny Computation: He computes the isogeny \(\phi_B: E_0 \rightarrow E_B := E_0/\langle R_B \rangle\).
  4. Public Key: Bob masks his output torsion points by computing \([\beta]\phi_B(P_A)\) and \([\beta]\phi_B(Q_A)\). He publishes his public key as \(pk_B = (E_B, [\beta]\phi_B(P_A), [\beta]\phi_B(Q_A))\).

2.3 Shared Key Computation

Upon receiving each other’s public keys, the parties use the masked points to recover the unmasked kernels for their respective second-step isogenies. Because the masking scalars are simply constant invertible multipliers over the entire torsion group, the subgroups generated by the mapped secret points remain unaffected.

Alice’s Computation:

  1. Upon receiving Bob’s public key \((E_B, [\beta]\phi_B(P_A), [\beta]\phi_B(Q_A))\), Alice uses her knowledge of \(R_A\) to linearly combine the received points and recover the subgroup \(\langle R'_A \rangle = \langle \phi_B(R_A) \rangle\).
  2. She computes the isogeny \(E_{AB} := E_B/\langle R'_A \rangle\).
  3. Her shared key is the \(j\)-invariant \(j(E_{AB})\).

Bob’s Computation:

  1. Upon receiving Alice’s public key \((E_A, [\alpha]\phi_A(P_B), [\alpha]\phi_A(Q_B))\), Bob uses his knowledge of \(R_B\) to linearly combine the received points and recover the subgroup \(\langle R'_B \rangle = \langle \phi_A(R_B) \rangle\).
  2. He computes the isogeny \(E_{BA} := E_A/\langle R'_B \rangle\).
  3. His shared key is the \(j\)-invariant \(j(E_{BA})\).

Due to the commutative nature of the isogeny kernels, \(j(E_{AB}) = j(E_{BA})\), meaning both parties derive the exact same value as their shared secret.

3 Security Assumptions

MD-SIDH relies on a modified version of the isogeny with torsion problem, defined as follows:

Let \(E_0/\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\) be a supersingular elliptic curve where \(\#E_0(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}) = (ABf)^2\), with \(E_0[B] = \langle P, Q \rangle\). Let \(A'\) be a uniformly random divisor of \(A\), and let \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}/B\mathbb{Z}^\times\) be a random scalar. Given a random \(A'\)-degree isogeny \(\phi: E_0 \rightarrow E_A\), finding \(\phi\) from the public tuple \((E_0, P, Q, E_A, [\alpha]\phi(P), [\alpha]\phi(Q))\) must be computationally infeasible.

Beyond the baseline assumptions of M-SIDH, the security of MD-SIDH critically depends on three specific conditions:

  • Degree Obfuscation: While the square-free part of the random divisor \(A'\) can be recovered using the Weil pairing, extracting the remaining prime power factors must be mathematically hard.
  • Unrestricted Scalar Masking: Hiding the torsion point images using any arbitrary invertible integer (\(\alpha\)) must not introduce new structural vulnerabilities, even though it fails to preserve the Weil pairing exactly.
  • Non-Cyclic Hardness: The core M-SIDH problem must still hold for non-cyclic isogenies. This is required because reducing an MD-SIDH instance (by removing the known square-free degree factors) naturally results in a non-cyclic M-SIDH instance.
