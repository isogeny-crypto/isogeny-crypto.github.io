terSIDH

ternary Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman

Published

July 9, 2026

Modified

July 10, 2026

1 Overview

The terSIDH protocol is a variant of the binSIDH protocol designed to significantly improve efficiency and reduce parameter sizes by utilizing variable-degree isogenies [BaFo23]. In the binSIDH protocol, computations are restricted to full-degree artificially oriented isogenies where the path is defined by a binary choice for each prime factor. The terSIDH protocol introduces a third option: for each prime factor, the protocol can choose to omit that component entirely, effectively skipping the isogeny step for that prime. This ternary approach yields approximately \(\log_2(3) \approx 1.6\) bits of security per prime compared to the single bit provided by binary choices, allowing the protocol to achieve target security levels with substantially smaller isogeny degrees and underlying primes.

2 Protocol Design

The terSIDH protocol modifies the binSIDH approach by relying on variable-degree artificially oriented isogenies. Below is a detailed walkthrough of the protocol setup, key generation, and shared key derivation.

2.1 Setup

The public parameters are generated as follows:

  • \(\lambda\): The security parameter (e.g., 128, 192, 256) that dictates the targeted security level.
  • \(t\): An integer depending on \(\lambda\), representing the number of distinct prime factors required to achieve the desired security. Because each prime provides \(\log_2(3) \approx 1.6\) bits of security, \(t\) is significantly smaller than in binSIDH (approximately \(\lambda / 1.6\)).
  • \(l_i, q_i\): Small, distinct prime numbers for \(i \in \{1, \dots, t\}\).
  • \(A, B\): Coprime integers where \(A = \prod_{i=1}^t l_i\) and \(B = \prod_{i=1}^t q_i\). \(A\) dictates the maximum possible degree of Alice’s isogenies, while \(B\) dictates Bob’s. The values are chosen such that \(A \approx B \approx \sqrt{p}\).
  • \(f\): A small cofactor used to ensure the resulting \(p\) is a valid prime.
  • \(p\): A large prime of the form \(p = ABf - 1\).
  • \(E_0\): A starting supersingular elliptic curve defined over \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\) with cardinality \(\#E_0(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}) = (p+1)^2\). To avoid special endomorphism ring properties that could lead to attacks, this curve should be generated via a uniformly random walk.
  • \(\mathfrak{A}, \mathfrak{B}\): Artificial orientations on \(E_0\). Specifically, the artificial \(A\)-orientation is defined as \(\mathfrak{A} = (G_1, G_2)\), where \(G_1, G_2 \subset E_0[A]\) are cyclic disjoint subgroups of order \(A\). The artificial \(B\)-orientation is similarly defined as \(\mathfrak{B} = (H_1, H_2)\), a pair of cyclic disjoint subgroups of order \(B\).

The globally public parameters are \((E_0, p, A, B, \mathfrak{A}, \mathfrak{B})\).

2.2 Key Generation

Both parties independently sample random ternary secret vectors that dictate the specific subgroups used to construct their secret isogenies. A choice of \(0\) corresponds to omitting the component for that prime. Consequently, the final isogeny has a variable degree that merely divides \(A\) or \(B\), rather than strictly equaling it.

Alice:

  1. Samples a secret vector \(\mathbf{a}\) uniformly at random from \(\{0, 1, 2\}^t\).
  2. Decomposes her artificial \(A\)-orientation \(\mathfrak{A} = (G_1, G_2)\). Since \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) are cyclic groups of order \(A = \prod_{i=1}^t l_i\), they can be written as the direct sum of their prime-order components: \(G_1 = G_1^1 \oplus \dots \oplus G_1^t\) and \(G_2 = G_2^1 \oplus \dots \oplus G_2^t\), where each \(G_1^i\) and \(G_2^i\) has order \(l_i\). She also explicitly sets \(G_0^i = \mathcal{O}\) (the point at infinity) for all \(i \in \{1, \dots, t\}\).
  3. Constructs the \(\mathfrak{A}\)-isogeny \(\phi_A : E_0 \to E_A\) defined by her secret vector \(\mathbf{a}\). For each \(i\)-th prime factor \(l_i\), she selects the kernel component from \(G_1^i\) (if \(\mathbf{a}_i = 1\)), \(G_2^i\) (if \(\mathbf{a}_i = 2\)), or \(\mathcal{O}\) (if \(\mathbf{a}_i = 0\)). Formally, \(\ker \phi_A = \langle G_{\mathbf{a}_1}^1, G_{\mathbf{a}_2}^2, \dots, G_{\mathbf{a}_t}^t \rangle\). Due to the possibility of \(\mathbf{a}_i = 0\), the actual degree of \(\phi_A\) divides \(A\).
  4. Computes the image of Bob’s artificial \(B\)-orientation \(\mathfrak{B}\) under her isogeny, yielding \(\mathfrak{B}' = \phi_A(\mathfrak{B}) = (\phi_A(H_1), \phi_A(H_2))\).
  5. Alice’s secret key is \(\mathbf{a}\) and her public key is \((E_A, \mathfrak{B}')\).

Bob:

  1. Samples a secret vector \(\mathbf{b}\) uniformly at random from \(\{0, 1, 2\}^t\).
  2. Decomposes his artificial \(B\)-orientation \(\mathfrak{B} = (H_1, H_2)\) into its prime-order components \(H_1^i\) and \(H_2^i\) of order \(q_i\). He similarly sets \(H_0^i = \mathcal{O}\) for all \(i \in \{1, \dots, t\}\).
  3. Constructs the \(\mathfrak{B}\)-isogeny \(\phi_B : E_0 \to E_B\). The kernel is chosen from \(\mathfrak{B}\) according to the vector \(\mathbf{b}\), such that \(\ker \phi_B = \langle H_{\mathbf{b}_1}^1, H_{\mathbf{b}_2}^2, \dots, H_{\mathbf{b}_t}^t \rangle\). The degree of \(\phi_B\) divides \(B\).
  4. Computes the image of Alice’s artificial \(A\)-orientation \(\mathfrak{A}\) under his isogeny, yielding \(\mathfrak{A}' = \phi_B(\mathfrak{A})\).
  5. Bob’s secret key is \(\mathbf{b}\) and his public key is \((E_B, \mathfrak{A}')\).

2.3 Shared Key Computation

Upon exchanging public keys, the parties compute their parallel isogenies to arrive at a common curve.

Alice:

  1. Receives Bob’s public key \((E_B, \mathfrak{A}')\). She verifies that \(\mathfrak{A}'\) is indeed a valid artificial \(A\)-orientation on \(E_B\); if not, she aborts.
  2. She computes the \(\mathfrak{A}'\)-isogeny \(\phi_A' : E_B \to E_{BA}\) defined by her ternary secret vector \(\mathbf{a}\). The degree of this isogeny divides \(A\).
  3. Her shared key is the \(j\)-invariant of the resulting curve, \(j(E_{BA})\).

Bob:

  1. Receives Alice’s public key \((E_A, \mathfrak{B}')\). He verifies that \(\mathfrak{B}'\) is a valid artificial \(B\)-orientation on \(E_A\); if not, he aborts.
  2. He computes the \(\mathfrak{B}'\)-isogeny \(\phi_B' : E_A \to E_{AB}\) defined by his ternary secret vector \(\mathbf{b}\). The degree of this isogeny divides \(B\).
  3. His shared key is the \(j\)-invariant of the resulting curve, \(j(E_{AB})\).

Due to the structure of parallel isogenies over artificially oriented curves, the resulting codomain curves \(E_{AB}\) and \(E_{BA}\) are isomorphic, ensuring \(j(E_{AB}) = j(E_{BA})\).

3 Security Assumptions

terSIDH shares its mathematical security assumptions with binSIDH but its reliance on variable-degree isogenies introduces unique implementation-level security challenges. Constructing constant-time implementations for terSIDH is inherently more difficult than for binSIDH due to the variable execution paths. Furthermore, designing zero-knowledge proofs for terSIDH public keys is particularly challenging because existing proof techniques inevitably leak the secret isogeny degree, potentially limiting terSIDH’s applicability in advanced cryptographic constructions compared to binSIDH.
